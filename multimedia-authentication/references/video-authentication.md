# Video authentication

This procedure is adapted from [SWGDE, *Best Practices for Digital Video Authentication*, 23-V-001-1.2 (7 March 2024), §§ 3-8](https://www.swgde.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/23-V-001-1.2-Best-Practices-for-Digital-Video-Authentication.pdf). Verify the current SWGDE version before relying on it in a new case. The document concerns digital video files; live streams, analog media, digital tape, and the truth of staged or reenacted events require other methods. It is not a substitute for examiner training, validated methods, or laboratory SOPs.

## Define the examination

Separate three possible objectives: **contextualization** (how the file reached its present state and what software last interacted with it), **source identification** (what device or equipment type produced it), and **content authentication** (whether encoded visual or audio samples or their timing changed). A camera-original file can still depict a staged event. Clarify which objective is requested, whether creation/modification time is disputed, the alleged recording system and custody, and whether suitable reference files or the device are available. Minimize task-irrelevant case information and document bias controls. If no suitable examination can answer the request, explain that limit and seek a narrower question or different discipline.

Determine whether the item is camera-native, an export, a screen recording, a proprietary recording-system file, or a platform derivative. Preserve the submitted container and timebase. Files received through a public portal or third party need particular scrutiny of their acquisition and generation before source claims. Obtain a known sample from the alleged device, or one that reproduces the alleged export/transfer history, when it could discriminate between explanations.

## Container, structure, attributes, and content

Inspect the container type and any proprietary validation features, filename convention, required player/codec, sidecar or peripheral streams, timecode, and block numbering. Preserve the native file even if an open-format export is needed; document what transcoding changes or omits. For open formats, compare binary structure and encoding patterns with suitably matched exemplars where source or processing history is disputed. Explore variations of relevant device settings and acquisition/export routes rather than treating a single exemplar as universal.

Inspect tracks and edit lists before assuming playback time equals capture time. Record embedded and tool-calculated attributes separately: dimensions, color, bitrate, codec/profile, sample sets, timestamps, rates, and durations. Metadata can change without affecting playback and is not sufficient alone. Use validated parsers; verify contested interpretations with a second tool or byte-level inspection.

Plan applicable **global** tests of the file as a whole and **local** tests of disputed intervals or regions. Distinguish direct observations, such as parsed metadata or visible scene changes, from measurements, such as duration or similarity, and address measurement error for the latter. Examine visual consistency systematically across the whole scene and candidate intervals. Depending on the proposition, test packet PTS/DTS and durations, missing or duplicated frames, GOP/keyframe structure, codec parameter changes, double compression, quantization or motion-vector changes, scene transitions, pixel-level alterations, frame correlation, sensor noise, and audio/video alignment. Use multiple relevant methods to cross-check material results; no single anomaly is determinative.

Variable frame rate, B-frame reordering, dropped frames during capture, stabilization, and intentional in-camera pause can produce apparent gaps. A hard cut is an editing observation, not proof of deceptive alteration. Sensor pattern methods such as PRNU depend on illumination, resolution, compression, and suitable exemplars; do not attribute an individual camera from a weak or class-level pattern.

Map every reported event to container time, stream timebase, frame or packet index, and a human-readable timecode. Distinguish decoded frame order from presentation order. Examine the intervals before and after a candidate edit and seek independent support such as corresponding audio discontinuity, changing sensor noise, container structure, or an earlier version. Check whether a codec or encoder transition is expected from ordinary export.

Frame or stream extraction is a derivative. Prefer stream copy for isolation; if impossible, use lossless or uncompressed conversion and verify decoded stream equivalence where feasible. Export still frames losslessly. Record FFmpeg command, seek behavior, pixel format, color handling, scaling, and whether extraction was frame-accurate. Retain representative neighboring frames and the original packet/timestamp data. Avoid relying on a media player's displayed time or interpolated frames. If an audio stream is present, conduct a separate audio-authentication analysis for claims about sound or audio/video integrity.

## Opinion and review

Explain the significance of supporting and contrary observations and how they answer the requested objective. Qualify conclusions for unknown generation, missing exemplars, tool and method limits, and measurement uncertainty. Useful result language is consistent with an original, inconsistent with an original, or inconclusive, always tied to the stated proposition and tests. Report any statistical model underlying a statistical result. Avoid absolute certainty except for a directly established alteration or deletion. Submit final analyses and conclusions for independent review by a comparably trained examiner; an agent cannot review its own work independently. Record disagreement resolution. Until review occurs, mark the opinion preliminary and unreviewed.
